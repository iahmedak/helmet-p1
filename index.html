01 / The glance

The problem was never where the screen sat.

A navigation screen asks for your eyes. That is the one thing a rider cannot spare.

Field note

This project began with a repeated sight: riders balancing a phone on the handlebar, looking down for the next turn, then back up at traffic. The observation is the starting point — not a safety study.

The first concept moved the screen into the helmet. A small display, a combiner optic, directions floating in the rider’s field of view. It sounded like progress. It was still a screen.

Every version of that idea carried the same cost: a glance away from the road. The display also demanded optics, brightness and eye relief that the team could not build well with the tools available. In November, the project changed direction.

The new question was narrower and more useful: can a route be understood by sound alone, without taking attention away from traffic? SOUNDNAV is the attempt to answer that with a bench prototype — and to be clear about what has not been proven.

01
Look down to confirm

The phone is the map, the compass and the next instruction. Each confirmation pulls the rider’s eyes off the road.

Observed behaviour · document in Week 4–5
02
Miss the turn, add a U-turn

A missed cue becomes a stop, a re-route and an unscheduled manoeuvre. Audio has to be early and unambiguous.

Conversation with peers · to verify
03
One ear plugged, less traffic heard

Riders already use one earbud for directions. That trades navigation attention for awareness of horns, speech and engines.

Design risk · needs testing

02 / The pivot

We built the wrong thing first. Then we changed it.

Early October 2026: a helmet HUD. 12 November 2026: an audio helmet. The pivot was forced by optics, and confirmed by the attention problem. Dates are the working record; confirm exact days in the field record.

Concept 01 · early Oct 2026

Helmet HUD

Abandoned
OPTICS STUDY / NO PHYSICAL HUD
  • Combiner alignment: the image drifted with every helmet fit and eye position.
  • Daylight brightness: a small projector could not compete with outdoor light.
  • Eye relief: the optic wanted a fixed viewing distance; a helmet does not offer one.
  • Attention: even when it worked, it was another screen to read.
Verdict STOP — the display was the wrong interface.

Concept 02 · 12 Nov 2026

Audio helmet

Bench build
AUDIO SYSTEM / REV 02
  • No screen: the route is carried by short, ordered cues instead of a map to read.
  • Buildable: ESP32, DFPlayer, MAX9814 and speakers are available; the optics were not.
  • Modular: the electronics sit in a rear bay, so the helmet shell can stay simple.
  • Still a risk: audio must stay intelligible in wind and traffic without masking either.
Verdict PROCEED — build the bench, test the cue language.

03 / System

A loop, not a chain.

Sound goes out; a command comes back. The intended build uses the microphone, the ESP32 and the phone in a loop. The bench build only closes the buzzer part of that loop.

Claim boundary

This is a student bench prototype. It does not prevent accidents, replace attention, or certify roadworthiness. It has not been tested in traffic or at riding speed. The site documents the concept, the bench build and the intended system so the next iteration can be judged honestly.

08 / Field notes

The most useful feedback was the uncomfortable kind.

A small group of classmates and peers saw the concept in November 2026. They did not validate safety. They did identify a usability gap.

Useful idea, but audio alone could be confusing. I would want to check the route somewhere.
Classmate · informal peer review · Nov 2026
Add a visual route on the phone. Not in the helmet — just something I can glance at when I stop.
Peer · informal peer review · Nov 2026
Open question How short is short enough?

We set an internal target of under two seconds per cue. It has not been tested in wind or traffic.

Team assumption · not peer feedback

PARAPHRASED FROM THE TEAM'S INFORMAL NOTES · NAMES, COUNT AND EXACT WORDING BELONG IN THE FIELD RECORD

Response

Kept audio primary; added a visual backup on the phone. The helmet still has no display. The phone screen is available before the ride and at a stop — not as a second thing to read while moving.

Limited the language to five cues and short phrases. The bench patterns were already minimal; the voice script was written to match. The U-turn cue stays deliberately sharper than the others.

This feedback is qualitative and informal. Names, dates and a count belong in the field record; they are not fabricated here.
